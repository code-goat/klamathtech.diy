# Making Is a Counter-Practice

Most of the technology in a young person's life is built to be consumed, not opened up. A phone, a game console, a streaming app — all of it is designed so the only verb available to you is "use." Common Sense Media's long-running census of tween and teen media habits puts entertainment screen time at somewhere between 50 and 60 hours a week depending on the year surveyed[^1] — more time than a full-time job, spent entirely on the consumption side of technology. We don't have a comparably solid source for how many of those same teenagers get to spend any real time *building* something with their hands instead — if you have one, we'd genuinely like to add it here — but the gap between "time spent consuming tech" and "time spent making things with it" doesn't need a precise percentage to be obvious. It shows up in how few people our age know how to open a computer, solder a joint, or diagnose why something stopped working instead of just replacing it.

That gap isn't an accident of personal choice. It's the intended outcome of a tech industry that makes far more money from your attention than from your competence. A maker space is a direct answer to that: a place built around the opposite premise, where the point isn't to hold your attention but to hand you tools.

## The Hacker Ethic, Applied to Hardware

We've written elsewhere about [the history of hacker culture]({{< ref "/info/hacker_manifesto/" >}}) — the MIT tinkerers, the free software movement, the conviction that systems should be open to understanding and improvement by anyone curious enough to try. Maker spaces are what that ethic looks like once it leaves the terminal and starts showing up in the physical world: soldering irons instead of just text editors, salvaged hardware instead of just source code, a workbench instead of just a repo.

Philosopher Ivan Illich had a name for the kind of tool this produces: "convivial tools" — tools that amplify what a person can do for themselves and their community, as opposed to industrial tools that turn people into dependents of whoever controls the tool.[^2] A 3D printer you understand and can repair is convivial. A sealed device you're contractually forbidden from opening is not. The difference isn't the technology — it's who's allowed to actually touch it.

## Maker Spaces as Community Infrastructure

A maker space — a hackerspace, a tool library, a community workshop, whatever you want to call the physical room where this happens — is infrastructure in the same sense a library or a well is infrastructure. It's a shared resource that makes individual self-sufficiency possible without requiring everyone to own every tool themselves. Hackerspaces as a formal movement trace back to European hacker collectives in the 1990s and spread from there,[^3] but the underlying idea — pooling tools and skill so a whole community can build and repair things together — is a lot older than the word "hackerspace."

For a bioregional collective like ours, that's not an abstract case to make. It's the same logic behind everything else we do: community-owned infrastructure beats individually-owned dependency, every time, because it's resilient in ways a single household's tool closet never will be.

## What This Looks Like Here

We're not just pointing at an abstract ideal — this is already underway. Our [Hack & Code Camp]({{< ref "/projects/code-camp/" >}}) teaches youth in the Klamath bioregion the hands-on fundamentals most schools skip entirely: soldering, hardware repair, installing an operating system, building a website, understanding the machine instead of just using it. And [UpRiv|Upcycle]({{< ref "/projects/upcycle/" >}}) is a maker space practice at a different scale — taking donated hardware that would otherwise be landfill, refurbishing it by hand, and turning it into community-owned infrastructure instead of individual e-waste.

Both are the same bet: that access to tools and the skill to use them is something a community should hold collectively, not something each person has to buy their way into alone.
